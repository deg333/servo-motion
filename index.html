    A servo is a motor inside a feedback loop

    A servo system measures what the motor is really doing and keeps correcting it. The motion controller (your PLC running the MC_ function blocks) sends a setpoint. The servo drive turns it into motor current, and an encoder on the motor reports the true position back.

    Inside the drive, three control loops are nested. Each outer loop produces the setpoint for the loop inside it, so the inner loops must always be the fastest.

    Position loopError in degrees becomes a velocity command (gain Kp).Slowest loop. Typically tens of Hz of bandwidth.
    Velocity loopSpeed error becomes a torque command (gain Kv, integral Ki).Typically a few hundred Hz.
    Current loopTorque is proportional to motor current (T = Kt × I). Field-oriented control keeps the current at the right angle.Fastest loop. Updates at the PWM rate, around 8 to 16 kHz or more.

    Tune the loops yourself

    The motor below follows a repeating 0° to 180° move. Raise the position gain Kp and the axis reacts faster, until it overshoots and rings. Add load inertia and the same gains become too aggressive, which is why drives need re-tuning when the machine changes.

    • Kp too high for the velocity loop: overshoot and oscillation.
    • Kv too high: noisy, buzzing torque and instability.
    • Ki helps against steady load torque but can overshoot.
    • A trapezoid profile (what MC_MoveAbsolute plans) is far gentler than a step.

    Why a brushless servo motor spins

    The rotor carries permanent magnets. The stator has three windings, A, B and C, 120° apart. The drive feeds them three sine-shaped currents, 120° out of step, so the stator's magnetic field is a vector that rotates.

    The drive keeps that field about 90° ahead of the rotor magnets, which gives the most torque per amp. Knowing the rotor angle for that is the main job of the encoder. This is commutation.

    Feedback: the encoder

    An incremental encoder is a slotted disc between a light source and two sensors, A and B, placed a quarter slot apart. Each slot edge gives a count. Because A and B are shifted by 90°, the order of the edges tells the direction. Counting all four edges per slot is called x4 decoding.

    A single index pulse (Z) once per turn gives a reference. Absolute and multi-turn encoders send the position as a number over a serial link, so no homing is needed after power-up. Resolvers are a rugged analogue alternative.

    Torque and speed limits

    A motor has a continuous torque it can hold without overheating and a higher peak torque for short bursts such as acceleration. At high speed the back-EMF eats the available bus voltage, so both fall. Drag the operating point to see which zone a move lands in. The curves are an example, not a specific motor.

    Sizing a servo, step by step

    Sizing means proving that a motor and drive can deliver the torque, speed and inertia your machine needs, with some margin. The load decides the motor, not the other way round.

    1. Describe the mechanism: mass, screw pitch or pulley radius, gearbox, friction, gravity, process force.
    2. Describe the move: distance, move time and cycle time. This sets speed and acceleration.
    3. Reflect the load to the motor shaft: inertia, friction and gravity torque.
    4. Find the torque in each phase: accelerate, cruise, decelerate, hold.
    5. Check peak torque, RMS torque, maximum speed and inertia ratio against the motor data, with a margin.
    6. Check the drive: peak current, regenerative energy and the position resolution you need.
    Peak torqueNeeded during acceleration and braking.Keep 20 to 30 % below the motor's peak at that speed.
    RMS torqueThe heating effect of the whole cycle, including the time spent holding.Must stay below the continuous rating.
    Inertia ratioLoad inertia seen by the motor, divided by rotor inertia.Often 5:1 or less for dynamic axes. A gearbox cuts it by the ratio squared.
    SpeedPeak speed of the move, including the gear ratio.Torque falls at high speed, so check the torque-speed curve.

      Things that catch people out

      Heavy loads on small motorsIf the inertia ratio is high, the axis rings or needs soft tuning. A gearbox or a larger-inertia motor fixes it.Try changing the gear ratio above and watch the motor list change.
      Vertical axesThe motor holds the load against gravity even at rest, so RMS torque includes the dwell time. Use a holding brake.Lowering the load returns energy to the drive.
      RegenerationBraking energy goes into the DC bus. Large inertia, fast stops or a falling load may need a brake resistor.Compare the energy per stop with the drive's data sheet.
      Duty and temperatureContinuous torque is rated at a given ambient temperature and mounting. A hot cabinet or small flange reduces it.Leave real margin for this.
      Mechanical stiffnessBacklash, belt stretch and coupling windup limit the gains you can use, whatever the motor size.The calculator does not model them.
      Drive currentThe drive must supply the peak current for the peak torque, for as long as the move lasts.Check its overload rating.

      Turning motor rotation into useful motion

      A servo motor gives rotation. A mechanism turns it into the motion the machine needs. Each one has a different transmission ratio, meaning how far the output moves for one radian of motor shaft. That ratio decides the force the motor can deliver and how much load inertia the motor feels.

      A rack and pinion has a constant ratio. Crank mechanisms have a ratio that changes with the crank angle, so torque and reflected inertia peak at certain positions. Change the link lengths below and watch the curves.

      Sliders change link lengths in millimetres. Where a crank cannot turn a full circle, the crank swings back and forth between its limits.

      Which one for a servo axis

      Rack and pinionLong strokes, constant ratio, easy to size. Backlash and tooth wear limit precision, so use a preloaded or helical pair for positioning.Pinion radius is the ratio: torque = force × radius.
      Crank and rockerGives an oscillating arc from continuous rotation. The motor can run at one speed while the rocker does a quick-return stroke.Keep the minimum transmission angle above about 40°.
      Crank and sliderGives a reciprocating stroke, as in presses, pumps and packaging pushers. Stroke is about twice the crank radius, and an offset makes the strokes unequal.Keep the rod at least 3 to 4 times the crank radius.
      Sizing with a varying ratioUse the worst case, which is the largest ratio for peak torque and the largest reflected inertia for the inertia ratio. Take the RMS torque over the whole cycle.The sizing page includes a rack and pinion option.
